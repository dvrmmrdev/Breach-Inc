Facilitator guide

Breach Inc. works well played alone, but works best when a leadership team plays it and then talks about what it would mean for them. This guide is for whoever runs that session: a CISO, a head of risk, a chief of staff or an L&D lead. You don't need to be a security expert.

What players go through

Players are a newly promoted security leader at ACME Inc., a fictional payments processor, through a major data breach: the weeks of secrecy before the announcement, the public fallout and the aftermath. Each decision takes days and moves four things: their influence, their team's health, their own health and, once the breach is public, public trust. At the end, the game shows how their choices played out, with lessons and questions to take back to their own organisation.

One playthrough takes about 35 minutes. There are no right answers, and different choices lead to different events and endings.

Choose a format

  • Play first, then talk (recommended), 60 minutes together. Everyone plays alone in the week before, and brings their ending and their list of questions. The session is all discussion. It suits busy executive teams, and nobody's choices are made in front of their boss.
  • Play together, 90 minutes. One screen, one facilitator. The room discusses each decision and votes, and the facilitator clicks the majority choice. Stop at the pause points below. It suits teams who'd respond together in a real incident.
  • Board briefing, 45 minutes. Board members play alone beforehand. In the session, take pause point 4 and the four board questions at the end of this guide.

Before the session

  • Send the link, breach-inc.com, with the time it takes and a note that it's fiction based on a real experience. It runs in any browser, with no account or download.
  • Ask players to use Email me the questions or Print or save as PDF on the last screen, and bring their list.
  • Find your own incident response plan, crisis communications plan and any contracts for outside help (forensics, legal, contact centres). You'll compare the game with them.
  • Read the care note below, and say it at the start.

A note on care

The game depicts burnout, secrecy from family and public abuse of staff. Some people in the room may have lived through a breach or a similar crisis. Say at the start that anyone can step out, and that nobody has to share their ending. Don't rank or compare endings: the point is the conversation, not the score.

Pause points

If you're playing together, stop at these points. If players played alone, use them as the agenda, in order.

1. After "The Briefing Room" (end of Act 0)

  • Who in our organisation would be told first, and who decides?
  • Who could the people who are told talk to, including about how they're coping?
  • Your first-week choice was security work or helping the business move faster. Which would our organisation have rewarded?

2. After "How Many People?" (end of Act 1)

  • If executives wanted updates in the first week, how would they get them without disrupting the response?
  • Could we list every internet-facing system we own, and who owns it, within a day?
  • Who signs off the number of people affected, and what would we do if it changed after we'd announced it?

3. After "The Share Sale"

  • What triggers a trading blackout here, who puts it in place, and how far does it reach?
  • Where would we send millions of worried people, and would they believe it was really us?
  • Who makes sure people go home in a six-week incident, and would they be listened to?

4. After "The CEO's Words" (end of Act 2)

  • Who in our organisation decides who outside the company is told before an announcement: the regulator, law enforcement, the national cyber security agency? Have they agreed it with Legal in advance?
  • Have we drafted the first statement our CEO would make? Who would argue for plain language, and would they be heard?
  • Which of the leaders who'd advise our board in a crisis have done it before?

5. After the ending

  • Without naming your ending, what was the decision you'd most like to take back, and why?
  • Which of the game's events would hurt our organisation most? Which are we least ready for?
  • Public trust only counted once the breach was public, but most of it was decided weeks before. What are we deciding now that the public will judge later?

Questions for a board

  • If our CEO and CIO were unavailable for a month, who could approve spending and sign off what we tell clients?
  • Which outside help do we already have on contract, and which would we be buying at crisis prices?
  • How would we know, during an incident, whether we're protecting our people at the expense of the public, or the other way round?
  • When did we last rehearse a breach we couldn't tell most of our own staff about?

Closing the session

End with actions, not impressions. Take the questions the group found hardest to answer, usually three to five, and agree for each one:

  • What we'll find out or change, in one sentence.
  • Who owns it: one named person, not a team.
  • When it's due, and where it will be reported back.

A good test of the session: a month later, can someone answer one of those questions who couldn't before?
