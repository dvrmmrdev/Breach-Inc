How to read this

The Night Desk has been internally assessed against a number of standards and regulator expectations, relating to board-level education, incident response exercises and governance principles. The results are outlined below; organisations should undertake their own review of coverage if The Night Desk is to be used to demonstrate compliance.

The Night Desk supports the incident response, recovery and business continuity parts of these standards strongly, and their wider governance parts only lightly. It rehearses how a leadership team leads an organisation through a ransomware attack and months of rebuilding. It does not assess, measure or improve an organisation's defences, and it doesn't test whether its backups or plans work.

The Night Desk is a 40 minute interactive story in which players, as a Head of IT Operations, recommend what a logistics company should do as ransomware stops its business. It ends with 11 lessons and 39 questions: 27 for business leaders and 12 for technology and cyber teams. The lessons are:

  1. Test your backups, and lock them down
  2. Someone has to be watching at night
  3. Decide in advance who can switch the company off
  4. The helpdesk is the front door
  5. Blame the person, and you lose the next warning
  6. Decide your position on paying before anyone asks
  7. Agree what comes back first
  8. Practise running on paper
  9. The money stops when invoicing stops
  10. Look after the people doing the rebuilding
  11. Share what you learned

Organisation licences add a facilitated session deck with notes for each pause point. The mapping below covers the game and the session together.

Each mapping uses four levels:

LevelMeaning
StrongThe Night Desk rehearses the requirement directly and generates questions that test it.
PartialThe Night Desk covers part of the requirement, or raises it without testing it.
LightThe Night Desk touches on the topic in passing. It is not evidence of meeting it.
Not coveredOut of scope. Use another resource.

No game or workshop makes an organisation compliant with any of these standards. The Night Desk is evidence of awareness, training and exercising, which several of them ask for.

NCSC Cyber Security Toolkit for Boards

The Night Desk covers one of the toolkit's ten modules strongly: Planning your response to cyber incidents. Unlike The Long Silence, that includes business continuity and disaster recovery. It covers seven more in part and touches the other two lightly. The toolkit is mostly about governance before an incident, and The Night Desk is mostly about leading through one, although its first act rehearses the decisions before the attack that shape everything afterwards.

Mapped against the toolkit's Questions for the board to ask about cyber security (version 3.0, reviewed April 2025).

UK Cyber Governance Code of Practice

The Night Desk supports Principle D (incident planning, response and recovery) strongly, and director training under Principle C. It does little for Principles A, B and E, which cover risk, strategy and oversight. The code was published by the government on 8 April 2025, and the NCSC Board Toolkit is organised around it.

Code actionDepthHow The Night Desk supports it
D1: a plan to respond to and recover from an incident affecting business critical servicesStrongThe questions test whether a plan covers the whole of a recovery: who can isolate systems, whether the plan survives the attack, what comes back first, working on paper, payroll, cash and insurance. It generates questions about your plan, but doesn't review it.
D2: exercise the plan at least once a year, with internal and external stakeholdersStrong, with limitsA Night Desk session is a leadership exercise, and the facilitator guide includes a board briefing format. It is a generic scenario, so it tests judgement more than your own plan. An organisation licence lets you adapt it to your policies and processes. It doesn't involve external stakeholders unless you invite them; your insurer, lawyers or key suppliers would be natural guests.
D3: in an incident, take responsibility for regulatory obligations, and support critical decisions and external communicationsStrong, with limitsLessons 3, 6, 7, 8 and 10: isolating systems, whether to pay, what comes back first, and what customers and staff are told. Regulatory reporting is the limit: lawyers notify the regulator in the background, and players don't decide it.
D4: post-incident review, with lessons fed backStrongLesson 5 and "A Name for the Board" test whether a review looks for conditions or culprits. Lesson 11 and "The Review" test who gets to read it, and whether it changes next year's plan. It doesn't cover how to run the review.
C3: directors train to improve their own cyber literacyStrongA board playing The Night Desk is director-level training. With an organisation licence, completions are recorded in a learning platform.
C4: an effective training and awareness programme, measured by suitable metricsPartialRecords completions through the learning platform package. It measures that people completed it, not changes in behaviour.
C1: a culture that encourages positive behaviours and accountabilityPartialLesson 5 is about whether people feel safe to report a mistake, and what the process does to them. Lesson 10 shows culture under strain.
C2: clear policies that support a positive cultureLightLesson 5 asks what your process would do to someone who was tricked. It doesn't review the policy.
A1: critical processes, information and services identified, prioritised and agreedPartialLesson 7 asks what would come back first, who has agreed that order, and which customers can least afford to wait.
A3: risk appetitePartialLessons 1 and 2 show resilience being traded against delivery and cost, and ask who makes that decision and whether it's recorded. Defining and communicating the appetite isn't covered.
B3: resources allocated effectively to manage the agreed risksPartialThe first act is a resourcing decision: engineers and money for backups and night monitoring, or for a flagship programme. The Overdraft revisits it when costs must be cut. It doesn't cover resourcing as a whole.
E1: a governance structure with clear roles, including executive and non-executive ownershipLightLessons 3 and 6 test who decides during an incident, not the standing structure.
A2, A4: senior risk ownership, supply chain resilienceLightLesson 1 asks who decides between resilience and delivery. Lesson 8 asks which suppliers depend on you, not how they could put you at risk.
E2: reporting and metricsLightLessons 1 and 2 suggest two useful measures: the last full restore, and unread alerts. Setting reporting, metrics and tolerances isn't covered.
A5, B1, B2, B4, E3 to E5: regular risk assessment, cyber strategy, dialogue with senior executives, audit, regulatory awarenessNot covered

NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF)

The Night Desk is useful evidence for one CAF outcome, D1.c testing and exercising. It is partial evidence for several others, because it raises backups, continuity, alert triage and identity checks. It asks whether these are in place, and shows what happens when they aren't, but it doesn't assess them. Most of the CAF is about technical and organisational controls. Mapped against CAF version 4.0 (published 18 April 2024, reviewed 30 October 2026).

CAF outcomeDepthHow The Night Desk supports it
D1.c Testing and exercisingStrong, with limitsA facilitated session is an exercise based on the kind of incident other organisations have experienced, and it runs through to the return of normal service. The CAF also expects scenarios drawn from your own experience or threat intelligence, which a generic scenario doesn't provide unless you adapt it with an organisation licence. It doesn't test a technical restoration.
D1.a Response planPartialThe 39 questions expose gaps in a plan, including how it connects to wider business plans. It doesn't check the plan against your essential functions.
D1.b Response and recovery capabilityPartialLesson 8 tests running at a reduced level without primary systems. Lesson 10 tests the few people a rebuild depends on and outside support, and lessons 1 and 3 test whether the information you'd need is available. Technical recovery isn't covered.
B5.a Resilience preparationPartialThe CAF lists tabletop exercises among ways to test business continuity and disaster recovery plans. A session can support that, and lesson 7 tests the order of recovery. It doesn't test the plans' practicality or completeness unless you compare them during the session; the facilitator guide suggests doing so.
B5.c BackupsPartialLesson 1 asks when a whole site was last restored, whether a copy is offline or unchangeable, and whether the account that runs the backups could destroy them. The story shows backups as the attackers' first target. The game doesn't examine your backups.
C1.c Generating alerts; C1.d Triage of security alertsPartialLesson 2 asks who looks at alerts at 2am on a Sunday, what they can do about them, and how many went unread last month. Tools and detection rules aren't covered.
B2.a Identity verification, authentication and authorisation; B2.d Identity and access managementPartialLesson 4 asks what a caller would need to know to get a password reset, and how you'd reset every account in the company. "Every Account" rehearses the choice of how to verify people. Access controls themselves aren't covered.
D2.a Post incident analysis; D2.b Using incidents to drive improvementsPartialThe CAF treats investigations focused solely on finding who to hold responsible as not achieved. Lesson 5 and "A Name for the Board" rehearse exactly that pressure, and lesson 11 asks whether a review would change next year's plan. It doesn't cover how to analyse root causes.
B6.a Cyber security culturePartialLesson 5 tests reporting and blame. Lesson 10 shows culture under strain.
B6.b Cyber security trainingPartialTraining for leaders, with completions recorded by the learning platform package. It is not technical training for the staff who run systems.
A1.b Roles and responsibilitiesPartialLessons 3 and 6 test who decides, and whether they know they can.
A1.c Decision-makingPartialLesson 1 asks how resilience and delivery are traded off, lesson 3 covers isolating systems, and lesson 6 covers the decision on paying.
B2.c Privileged user management; B5.b Design for resilienceLightThe attackers use an administrator's keys. Lesson 3 asks whether each site can be cut off separately, and lesson 7 asks whether you could build a clean network. Neither is assessed.
Rest of Objectives A, B and C, including supply chain, data security and threat huntingNot covered

Sources: CAF principles A1, B2, B5, B6, C1, D1 and D2, NCSC.

NHS Data Security and Protection Toolkit (DSPT)

The Night Desk is a good fit for the CAF-aligned DSPT's D1.c, which lists tabletop exercises, and its plans are expected to cover ransomware. Several of its questions match what the DSPT expects a plan to contain, especially where the plan must still work during an outage. The story's company delivers medicines and supplies to pharmacies and care homes, so care appears as a dependency, not as the service being run. The CAF-aligned DSPT applies to larger NHS organisations, such as trusts, since 2024–25. Other organisations, including most GP practices and social care providers, use the standards-based DSPT until their requirements move to the CAF.

DSPT outcomeDepthHow The Night Desk supports it
D1.c Testing and exercisingStrong, with limitsThe DSPT lists tabletop exercises among exercise types, and a Night Desk session is a tabletop exercise. The DSPT also expects scenarios drawn from your own risk assessment, and findings recorded as time-bound actions. A generic scenario needs adapting (organisation licence), and the session's action list is a start on your own follow-up.
D1.a Response planPartialThe DSPT expects plans to cover ransomware, and to stay available during an outage, for example on paper. Lessons 1 and 3 test exactly that. Patient-care impacts and safe-care workarounds aren't covered, though "The Care Home" raises what happens to the most vulnerable people while you recover.
D1.b Response and recovery capabilityPartialThe DSPT lists paper-based processes, duplicated key roles, and supplier support agreed in advance. Lessons 8 and 10 test all three. Clinical fallbacks and technical recovery aren't covered.
B5.a Resilience preparationPartialThe DSPT expects you to know what to restore first and in what order, with clinical input where care is affected. Lesson 7 tests agreeing that order. It doesn't cover the system dependencies or the clinical input.
B5.c BackupsPartialThe DSPT expects at least one offline copy, and restore tests that represent the live service. Lesson 1 asks about both, including restoring a whole site. Recovery objectives and documentation aren't covered.
D2 Lessons learnedPartialAs for the CAF.
B6 Staff awareness and trainingPartialAs for the CAF: leadership training with recorded completions.

NHS organisations can buy The Night Desk at the charity and NHS price.

Sources: CAF-aligned DSPT guidance, principles B5 and D1, NHS England (last edited 15 October 2026).

UK GDPR personal data breach reporting

The Night Desk rehearses one part of UK GDPR breach handling closely: telling people their own data was stolen, and protecting them from what follows. In this story, those people are the company's own staff. It says little about reporting to the regulator. The story does mention the 72-hour rule, but the lawyers deal with it in the background, and it isn't a decision for players. It doesn't teach the legal tests.

ICO expectationDepthHow The Night Desk supports it
Tell people affected without undue delay when the risk is high, in plain language, with advice on protecting themselves (Article 34)Strong"Telling Them" asks how and when to tell staff, including whether to wait until the investigation confirms whose data was taken. "What They Took" shows the fraud that follows, and asks how to warn and protect people. Lessons 6 and 10 ask how and how quickly you'd do it.
A response plan, with a dedicated person or team, and staff who know how to escalatePartialLesson 5 asks whether the night shift would ring in a mistake, and lessons 3 and 6 test who decides. It doesn't cover the plan's contents for personal data.
Report to the ICO without undue delay, within 72 hours, and in phases if the investigation isn't finished (Articles 33(1) and 33(4))LightThe story shows lawyers notifying the regulator inside 72 hours, "with what little anyone knew". That is a phased report, but players don't decide it, and no question asks about it. The session deck raises notification requirements.
Assess the risk to individuals to decide whether to report or tell themLight"Telling Them" raises whether to wait for certainty. It doesn't teach the assessment.
Recognise that loss of access to personal data can be a breach, not only loss or theftLightThe story shows payroll and HR systems going dark, and the harm that causes. It doesn't treat that as a question of reporting.
Investigate the causes, including human error, and stop it happening againPartialLessons 4, 5 and 11 cover why a person could be tricked, and what would change. It doesn't cover the ICO's expectations.

Source: Personal data breaches: a guide, ICO (latest update 20 August 2025).

ISO/IEC 27001:2022

The Night Desk can be evidence for ISO/IEC 27001 awareness, incident preparation and continuity controls, mostly as part of a wider programme. An auditor will look for your own procedures, tests and records. The Night Desk supports those but doesn't replace them.

5.24 Incident management planning and preparationPartialThe questions test whether responsibilities and procedures cover isolating systems, who decides on a ransom, and how the plan survives an attack.
5.29 Security during disruption; 5.30 ICT readiness for business continuityPartialLessons 7 and 8 test what comes back first and how the business runs without systems, and "Every Account" shows how security checks hold up under pressure. They don't test your actual readiness.
8.13 Information backupPartialLesson 1 asks about restore tests and offline or unchangeable copies. It doesn't examine your backups.
5.16 Identity management; 5.17 Authentication informationPartialLesson 4 tests how the helpdesk checks a caller before resetting a password, and how you'd reissue every login.
6.3 Awareness, education and training; clause 7.3 AwarenessPartialLeadership awareness training with recorded completions. It isn't role-based security training for all staff.
6.8 Information security event reportingPartialLesson 5 asks whether staff would report a mistake. It doesn't cover the reporting mechanism.
5.27 Learning from incidentsPartialLessons 5 and 11 ask how a review looks at conditions, not culprits, and whether it would change next year's plan.
8.16 Monitoring activitiesLightLesson 2 asks who watches at night, and what they can do. It doesn't cover the monitoring itself.

NACD Director's Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight (US)

The Night Desk maps strongly to the handbook's Tool A, the board's role in ransomware preparedness and response, and Tool B, the board's role in cyber incident response. Tool A suggests directors take part in or observe tabletop exercises that simulate a ransomware event, and The Night Desk is a ready-made one. It does less for the six principles' work on strategy, measurement and reporting. The 2026 edition was published in April 2026 by NACD with the Internet Security Alliance, with a foreword from CISA.

Handbook principle or toolDepthHow The Night Desk supports it
Tool A: The Board's Role in Ransomware Preparedness and ResponseStrongTool A's central governance question is who decides whether to pay a ransom. "The Board" and lesson 6 rehearse that decision, including legal checks, alternatives such as restoring from backups, and what insurance covers. Tool A also asks whether the organisation could withstand an extended outage, whether redundancies have been tested, and how risk transfer such as cyber insurance fits (lessons 1, 7, 8 and 9). It lists retainers for response support (lesson 10). Quantified risk models aren't covered, the sanctions law is American in the handbook and generic in the game, and vendors as a route in aren't covered.
Tool B: The Board's Role in Cyber Incident ResponseStrongTool B's pillars are governance, preparedness, response and recovery. The Night Desk rehearses all four at leadership level. It covers customers, staff, insurers, banks and law enforcement, and the recovery as a months-long programme. Regulators and the media appear in the story, but players make fewer decisions about them than in The Long Silence. It doesn't cover technical containment.
Principle 1: Treat Cybersecurity as a Strategic RiskPartialThe story shows ransomware threatening the survival of the company, through cash, contracts and insurance. Lesson 9 asks how many weeks you could keep going without invoicing.
Principle 4: Adopt an Enterprise Framework for Managing Cyber RiskPartialThe handbook says leading boards take part in tabletop simulations that test decision-making under stress. A Night Desk session is one. Lessons 1 and 2 raise the trade-off between resilience and cost. Resilience metrics aren't covered.
Principle 6: Encourage Systemic Resilience and CollaborationPartialLesson 11 asks what you could share with peers, and whether you're part of a group who'd tell each other what they learned. Lesson 8 asks whether you'd ask a competitor for help. Tabletop exercises with critical suppliers aren't raised.
Principle 2: Monitor Legal and Disclosure ImplicationsLightLesson 6 asks how you'd check whether a payment was legal, and lesson 9 raises insurance answers. The game doesn't mention SEC rules or materiality tests.
Principle 3: Establish Board Oversight Structures and Access to ExpertiseLightThe story brings in a negotiator, investigators and lawyers. It doesn't cover board structures.
Principle 5: Guide Cybersecurity Risk Measurement and ReportingLightLessons 1 and 2 suggest measures: the last full restore, and unread alerts. Reporting isn't covered.

Sources: Director's Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight, 2026 and its press release, NACD.

World Economic Forum Principles for Board Governance of Cyber Risk

The Night Desk supports these principles lightly to partly. The WEF principles are about strategy, economics and organisational design, and say little about leading an incident. The closest matches are Principle 3's call for tested plans for adverse cyber events, and Principle 2, because the story turns on what an attack costs. The WEF published them in March 2021 with NACD and the Internet Security Alliance. NACD's 2026 handbook now builds on them, so for most boards the NACD mapping above is more useful.

WEF principleDepthHow The Night Desk supports it
3. Align cyber-risk management with business needsPartialBoards should require written and tested plans for adverse cyber events. The Night Desk tests a leadership team against the kind of event those plans are for, and its questions show where a plan is thin. Lessons 1 and 2 show resilience competing with the business's priorities.
2. Understand the economic drivers and impact of cyber riskPartialLesson 9 asks about cash, credit and insurance. Lesson 2 asks what a cut to monitoring would cost. It shows the impact on one company, not the economics of cyber risk, and it doesn't quantify your risk.
5. Incorporate cybersecurity expertise into board governancePartialTraining builds directors' cyber knowledge, and The Night Desk is director-level training.
6. Encourage systemic resilience and collaborationPartialLesson 11 covers sharing what you learned with peers, and the story shows how an attack spreads harm to customers, suppliers and staff's families.
1. Cybersecurity is a strategic business enablerLightThe story shows ransomware as an enterprise issue, not an IT matter.
4. Ensure organisational design supports cybersecurityLightLessons 2, 3 and 10 expose the cover, authority and staffing gaps an attack reveals. Organisational design itself isn't covered.

Sources: Principles for Board Governance of Cyber Risk, World Economic Forum; principle titles checked against the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance summary.
