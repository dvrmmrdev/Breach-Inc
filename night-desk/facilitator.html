Facilitator guide: The Night Desk

The Night Desk works well played alone, but works best when a leadership team plays it and then talks about what it would mean for them. This guide is for whoever runs that session: a CIO, a head of risk or resilience, a chief of staff or an L&D lead. You don't need to be a security expert, and nor do your players: most of the big calls in the story belong to the Chief Executive, the Finance Director, Legal and the board.

What players go through

Players are the Head of IT Operations at ACME Logistics, a fictional regional distributor of medicines and food, through a ransomware attack in its busiest week of the year: the night the screens go black, the ransom demand, the weeks of running on paper, the cash running out, and the months of rebuilding. Most decisions are recommendations to the people who own them. Whether the room listens depends on the player's influence, and the game shows when they're overruled.

Each decision takes time and moves four things: their influence, their team's health, their own health and how much of the company's service is running. From the board meeting on, a fifth clock counts down the company's liquidity. At the end, the game shows how their choices played out, with lessons and questions for tech and cyber teams and for business leaders.

One playthrough takes about 40 minutes. There's no perfect path, and different choices lead to different events and endings.

Choose a format

  • Play first, then talk (recommended), 60 minutes together. Everyone plays alone in the week before, and brings their ending and their list of questions. The session is all discussion. It suits busy executive teams, and nobody's choices are made in front of their boss.
  • Play together, 90 minutes. One screen, one facilitator. The room discusses each decision and votes, and the facilitator clicks the majority choice. Stop at the pause points below. It suits teams who'd respond together in a real incident.
  • Board briefing, 45 minutes. Board members play alone beforehand. In the session, take pause points 3 and 4: paying the ransom, and the cash. The session deck adds questions written for boards.

Before the session

  • Send the link, breach-inc.com/night-desk, with the time it takes and a note that it's fiction. It needs no account or download, and it's best played in a browser on a laptop or desktop.
  • Check that the link opens on your company's workstations, unless you're hosting the game internally.
  • Ask players to use Email me the questions, Copy the questions or Print or save as PDF on the last screen, and bring their list.
  • Find, or ask for, four documents you'll compare the game with: your incident response plan, the date and result of your last full restore test, your cyber insurance policy and its last renewal questionnaire, and your business continuity plan for running without systems.

Pause points

If you're playing together, stop at these points. If players played alone, use them as the agenda, in order.

  1. After "Nobody Watching at Night" (the last decision in Act 0)
  2. After "Pull the Plug" (the only decision in Act 1)
  3. After "The Board"
  4. After "Headlights in the Darkness" (end of Act 3)
  5. After the ending

The full facilitator notes and the session deck, with questions and conversation points for each pause point, come with an organisation licence.

Closing the session

End with actions, not impressions. Take the questions the group found hardest to answer, usually three to five, and agree for each one:

  • What we'll find out or change, in one sentence.
  • Who owns it: one named person, not a team.
  • When it's due, and where it will be reported back.

A good test of the session: a month later, can someone answer one of those questions who couldn't before? For The Night Desk, the best single test is simple: when did we last restore a whole site, and are our backups locked down?
