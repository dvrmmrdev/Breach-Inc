How to read this

Breach Inc. has been internally assessed against a number of standards and regulator expectations, relating to board-level education, incident response exercise and governance principles. The results are outlined below; organisations should undertake their own review of coverage if Breach Inc. is to be used to demonstrate compliance.

Breach Inc. supports the incident response parts of these standards strongly and their wider governance parts only lightly. It rehearses how a leadership team responds to a major data breach. It does not assess, measure or improve an organisation's defences.

Breach Inc. is a 35 minute interactive story in which players lead the response to a major data breach. It ends with 11 lessons and 29 questions for the leadership team. The lessons are:

  1. Foundations, automation and rehearsal
  2. Who's read in, and who they can talk to
  3. Scope, and the number
  4. Leadership: one channel, and clear decision-makers
  5. Insider risk
  6. Decisions only the board can make
  7. A public response people can trust
  8. Help on contract before you need it
  9. Someone has to make rest happen
  10. Tell your own people first
  11. The crisis doesn't end at the announcement

Organisation licences add a facilitated session deck with notes for each pause point. The mapping below covers the game and the session together.

Each mapping uses four levels:

LevelMeaning
StrongBreach Inc. rehearses the requirement directly and generates questions that test it.
PartialBreach Inc. covers part of the requirement, or raises it without testing it.
LightBreach Inc. touches on the topic in passing. It is not evidence of meeting it.
Not coveredOut of scope. Use another resource.

No game or workshop makes an organisation compliant with any of these standards. Breach Inc. is evidence of awareness, training and exercising, which several of them ask for.

NCSC Cyber Security Toolkit for Boards

Breach Inc. covers one of the toolkit's ten modules strongly: Planning your response to cyber incidents. It covers six more in part and touches the rest lightly. The toolkit is mostly about governance before an incident, and Breach Inc. is about leading during one. The two work well together: the toolkit sets the questions, and Breach Inc. makes a board feel why they matter.

Mapped against the toolkit's Questions for the board to ask about cyber security (version 3.0, reviewed April 2025).

UK Cyber Governance Code of Practice

Breach Inc. supports Principle D (incident planning, response and recovery) strongly, and director training under Principle C. It does little for Principles A, B and E, which cover risk, strategy and oversight. The code was published by the government on 8 April 2025, and the NCSC Board Toolkit is organised around it.

Code actionDepthHow Breach Inc. supports it
D2: exercise the plan at least once a year, with internal and external stakeholdersStrong, with limitsA Breach Inc. session is a leadership exercise, and the facilitator guide includes a board briefing format. It is a generic scenario, so it tests judgement more than your own plan. An organisation licence lets you adapt it to your policies and processes. It doesn't involve external stakeholders unless you invite them, and lesson 1 asks whether your rehearsals include key suppliers.
D3: in an incident, take responsibility for regulatory obligations, and support critical decisions and external communicationsStrongLessons 3, 6, 7 and 10: signing off and revising the number affected, contacting regulators, the CEO's statement, the public response, and staff and media communications.
D1: a plan to respond to and recover from an incidentPartialThe 29 questions test whether a plan covers who's read in, deputies, approval of spending, surge capacity and communications. Recovery of systems isn't covered.
D4: post-incident review, with lessons fed backPartialLesson 11 asks how the team would review a breach without blame, and make sure the lessons change the plan and the next exercise. It doesn't cover how to run the review.
C3: directors train to improve their own cyber literacyStrongA board playing Breach Inc. is director-level training. With an organisation licence, completions are recorded in a learning platform.
C4: an effective training and awareness programme, measured by suitable metricsPartialRecords completions through the learning platform package. It measures that people completed it, not changes in behaviour.
C1: a culture that encourages positive behaviours and accountabilityLightLessons 9, 10 and 11 show culture under strain.

A1: critical processes, information and services identifiedLightLesson 1 asks how quickly you could list internet-facing systems and their owners.
A4: supply chain resilienceLightLesson 8 covers suppliers as surge help, not as a risk.

Defining and communicating the appetite isn't covered.
B1 to B4: cyber strategy and resourcingNot coveredLesson 8 asks who can approve unbudgeted spending in a crisis, which is a crisis-time version of B3.
E2 to E5: reporting and metrics, audit, regulatory awarenessLight for E2, otherwise not coveredLesson 1 and the session deck ask which measures would show that foundational controls are working. Setting reporting, metrics and tolerances isn't covered.

NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF)

Breach Inc. is useful evidence for one CAF outcome, D1.c testing and exercising. It is partial evidence for staff training (B6.b) and incident-time roles and decisions (A1.b, A1.c). Most of the CAF is about technical and organisational controls, which Breach Inc. doesn't assess. Mapped against CAF version 4.0 (published 18 April 2024, reviewed 6 August 2025).

CAF outcomeDepthHow Breach Inc. supports it
D1.c Testing and exercisingStrong, with limitsA facilitated session is an exercise based on a real-world style incident. The CAF also expects scenarios drawn from your own threat intelligence and risk assessment, which a generic scenario doesn't provide unless you adapt it with an organisation licence.
D1.a Response planPartialThe 29 questions expose gaps in a plan (who's read in, deputies, spending approval, surge capacity, communications). It doesn't check the plan against your essential functions.
B6.b Cyber security trainingPartialTraining for leaders, with completions recorded by the learning platform package. It is not technical training for the staff who run systems.
A1.b Roles and responsibilitiesPartialLessons 2 and 4 test who decides, who's read in and who escalates during an incident.
A1.c Decision-makingPartialLesson 6 covers decisions only the board can make, and lesson 8 covers delegated spending authority in a crisis.
B6.a Cyber security cultureLightLessons 9, 10 and 11 show culture under strain.
D2.a and D2.b Lessons learnedPartialLesson 11 asks how the team would review a breach without blame and feed the lessons into plans and exercises (D2.b). It doesn't cover root cause analysis (D2.a).
D1.b Response and recovery capabilityLightLesson 8 asks how you'd scale up analysts and call handlers. Technical recovery isn't covered.
Objectives B (except B6) and C: protecting systems, detecting eventsNot covered

Sources: CAF principles A1, B6, D1 and D2, NCSC.

NHS Data Security and Protection Toolkit (DSPT)

Breach Inc. is a good fit for the CAF-aligned DSPT's D1.c, which lists tabletop exercises and data breach scenarios. Several of its questions match what the DSPT expects a response plan to contain. The CAF-aligned DSPT applies to larger NHS organisations, such as trusts, since 2024–25. Other organisations, including most GP practices and social care providers, use the standards-based DSPT until their requirements move to the CAF.

DSPT outcomeDepthHow Breach Inc. supports it
D1.c Testing and exercisingStrong, with limitsThe DSPT lists tabletop exercises and communications tests with the media and public among exercise types. A Breach Inc. session is a tabletop exercise built on a data breach. The DSPT also expects scenarios drawn from your own risk profile and threat intelligence, and findings recorded as time-bound actions. A generic scenario needs adapting (organisation licence) and your own follow-up.
D1.a Response planPartialThe DSPT expects plans to include key contacts with backups, roles, escalation and regulatory reporting thresholds. Breach Inc.'s questions on deputies (lesson 4), who's read in (lesson 2) and who tells the regulator (lesson 6) test exactly these. Patient-care impacts and safe-care workarounds aren't covered.
D1.b Response and recovery capabilityPartialThe DSPT expects key roles to be duplicated, and support from suppliers and partners to be agreed in advance. Lessons 4 and 8 test both. Technical recovery and paper-based fallbacks aren't covered.
D2 Lessons learnedPartialAs for the CAF.
B6 Staff awareness and trainingPartialAs for the CAF: leadership training with recorded completions.

NHS organisations can buy Breach Inc. at the charity and NHS price.

Source: CAF-aligned DSPT guidance, principle D1, NHS England (last edited 15 October 2026).

ISO/IEC 27001:2022

Breach Inc. can be evidence for ISO/IEC 27001 awareness and incident preparation controls, mostly as part of a wider programme. An auditor will look for your own incident procedures and records. Breach Inc. supports those but doesn't replace them.

Clause or Annex A controlDepthHow Breach Inc. supports it
5.24 Incident management planning and preparationPartialThe questions test whether responsibilities and procedures cover who's read in, deputies, spending approval and communications.
5.5 Contact with authoritiesPartialLesson 6 asks who contacts the regulator, police and national cyber agency, and when, agreed with Legal in advance.
6.3 Awareness, education and training; clause 7.3 AwarenessPartialLeadership awareness training with recorded completions. It isn't role-based security training for all staff.
Clause 5.1 Leadership and commitmentLightShows leaders why their involvement matters. It isn't evidence of commitment in itself.
5.26 Response to incidentsLightRehearses leadership decisions, not technical response procedures.
5.27 Learning from incidentsPartialLesson 11 asks how lessons from a no-blame review would change plans and the next exercise.

5.25 Assessment of events; 5.28 Collection of evidenceNot covered

Control numbers and titles are from ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A, which is sold by ISO and isn't freely available.

UK GDPR personal data breach reporting

Breach Inc. rehearses the leadership decisions behind UK GDPR breach reporting: when to tell the regulator, how sure you need to be of the numbers, and how to tell the people affected. It doesn't teach the legal tests or deadlines. The story isn't set in a particular country, so the 72-hour rule never appears.

ICO expectationDepthHow Breach Inc. supports it
Report to the ICO without undue delay, within 72 hours, and in phases if the investigation isn't finished (Articles 33(1) and 33(4))PartialLesson 3 is about announcing a cautious estimate or waiting for an exact number, and what happens when the number changes. That is the dilemma phased reporting addresses. Lesson 6 asks who contacts the regulator and when.
Tell people affected without undue delay when the risk is high, in plain language, with advice on protecting themselves (Article 34)StrongLesson 7 covers a public response people can trust: where to send millions of worried people, and whether they'd believe it was really you.
A response plan, with a dedicated person or team, and staff who know how to escalatePartialLessons 2 and 4 test who's read in and who decides.
Plan for more data subject requests after a breachPartialLesson 8 asks whose number you'd ring for a thousand extra call handlers.
Document every breach, including the facts, effects and remedial action (Article 33(5))Not covered
Assess the risk to individuals to decide whether to reportNot covered

Source: Personal data breaches: a guide, ICO (latest update 20 August 2025).

NACD Director's Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight (US)

Breach Inc. maps strongly to the handbook's Tool B, the board's role in cyber incident response. It is a ready-made version of the tabletop exercise the handbook recommends under Principles 4, 5 and 6. It does little for the six principles' work on strategy, measurement and reporting. The 2026 edition was published in April 2026 by NACD with the Internet Security Alliance, with a foreword from CISA.

Handbook principle or toolDepthHow Breach Inc. supports it
Tool B: The Board's Role in Cyber Incident ResponseStrongTool B lists the stakeholders to contact: regulators, law enforcement, insurers, customers, investors, the media, banks and credit bureaus. Breach Inc. rehearses most of them (lessons 6, 7 and 10). Tool B also covers pre-drafted holding statements (lesson 6), pre-negotiated retainers for response services (lesson 8), and the warning that what's known changes during an incident (lesson 3). Lesson 6 and the session deck add insurers to the outsiders to tell. It doesn't cover backups or technical containment.
Principle 2: Monitor legal and disclosure implicationsPartialThe handbook asks boards to approve escalation, materiality and disclosure processes before a crisis, and to review simulations that test readiness for deadlines like the SEC's four business days. Lessons 3 and 6 test the people side of this: who signs off the number and the statement. Lesson 5 covers trading blackouts. The game doesn't mention SEC rules or materiality tests.
Principle 4: Adopt an enterprise frameworkPartialThe handbook says leading boards take part in tabletop simulations that test decision-making under stress. A Breach Inc. session is one. Lesson 8 asks which disruptions the board has agreed it could accept, which raises risk appetite. Resilience metrics aren't covered.
Principle 5: Guide risk measurement and reportingLightThe handbook suggests tabletop exercises with executives and directors to test response coordination and crisis communication. Lesson 1 asks which measures would show the foundations are working. Reporting isn't covered.
Principle 6: Encourage systemic resilience and collaborationPartialLesson 6 asks who contacts law enforcement and the national cyber agency. Lesson 1 asks whether rehearsals include key suppliers, matching the handbook's question on tabletop exercises with critical suppliers.
Principle 3: Board oversight structures and access to expertiseLightLesson 6 asks which leaders would advise the board in a crisis and whether they've done it before.
Principle 1: Treat cybersecurity as a strategic riskLightThe story shows a breach reaching revenue, reputation and trust, not just IT.

Sources: Director's Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight, 2026 and its press release, NACD.

World Economic Forum Principles for Board Governance of Cyber Risk

Breach Inc. supports these principles only lightly. The WEF principles are about strategy, economics and organisational design, and say little about leading an incident. The closest match is Principle 3's call for tested plans for adverse cyber events. The WEF published them in March 2021 with NACD and the Internet Security Alliance. NACD's 2026 handbook now builds on them, so for most boards the NACD mapping above is more useful.

WEF principleDepthHow Breach Inc. supports it
3. Align cyber-risk management with business needsPartialBoards should require written and tested plans for adverse cyber events. Breach Inc. tests a leadership team against the kind of event those plans are for, and its questions show where a plan is thin.
5. Incorporate cybersecurity expertise into board governancePartialTraining builds directors' cyber knowledge, and Breach Inc. is director-level training. Lesson 6 asks whether the board's advisers have done this before.
1. Cybersecurity is a strategic business enablerLightThe story shows a breach as an enterprise issue, not an IT matter.
4. Ensure organisational design supports cybersecurityLightLessons 2, 4 and 9 expose the ownership, authority and staffing gaps a breach reveals. Organisational design itself isn't covered.
6. Encourage systemic resilience and collaborationLightLesson 6 covers contacting law enforcement and the national cyber agency. The session deck's NotPetya example shows collateral damage across organisations.
2. Understand the economic drivers and impact of cyber riskNot covered

Sources: Principles for Board Governance of Cyber Risk, World Economic Forum; principle titles checked against the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance summary.
